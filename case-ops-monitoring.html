Catching a failing payment route before customers do

A real-time ops console that monitors success rates for every processor — down to each MNO wallet and bank — and reroutes to a funded backup when a route degrades, with ops in control of the switch.

The problem

A cross-border business routes transactions through many processors, and each processor fulfils to many destinations — mobile-money wallets (M-Pesa, Airtel, MTN) and banks (UBA Kenya, Ecobank Kenya). When a processor, a single MNO, or a single bank starts failing, money stops moving — and operations usually find out from customer complaints, not from a system.

There's no single real-time view of success rate per processor, let alone per destination; no agreed line for when to act; and no fast, safe way to move traffic to a backup. The result is slow detection, manual firefighting, and failed transactions exactly when reliability matters most.

How might ops see a route failing in real time — down to the specific bank or wallet — and reroute to a healthy, funded backup in seconds, without handing a machine the keys entirely?

My role

I designed the monitoring hierarchy, the two-threshold model, and the failover logic — including the funding check and the authorise/override flow that keeps a human on the switch. The artifacts here are the PRD, a working real-time console with the full failover flow, and the system architecture.

Approach

  1. Monitored at three levelsProcessor → service (country × transaction type × payment method) → destination (MNO wallet or bank). A processor reading "80%" is really one bank failing — and the drill-down shows which, so ops diagnose instead of guess.
  2. Separated "tell me" from "act"Two ops-set thresholds, not one: an alert threshold that notifies the team to review, and a lower failover threshold that triggers an automated response. They are deliberately different bars.
  3. Made failover safe by constructionOn a failover breach the system checks two things before proposing anything: is a backup configured for this exact service, and is it adequately funded? Never route to a processor that can't take the volume.
  4. Kept a human on the switchThe system proposes; ops authorise, override to stay, or switch manually at any time. Automation does the detecting and the checking; a person owns the decision.
  5. Showed the trend, not just the momentAn hourly chart with the thresholds drawn on it, so gradual degradation is visible before it becomes an incident.

The failover decision

Rate < failover threshold → backup configured? (no → manual) → backup funded? (no → manual) → propose switch → ops authorise / override → reroute & log. A manual switch is available at any time, and ops can switch back once the primary recovers.

The guardrail that makes it shippable: the system never switches onto a processor that can't take the volume, and never switches without a human — detection and checks are automated, the decision stays with ops.
